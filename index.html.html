Senior customers generated the highest revenue ($12.3M), followed by Adults ($9.6M), with Youth well behind ($3.3M) — meaning older segments are the actual revenue drivers, not the age group most campaigns default to targeting. Revenue was also heavily concentrated: the top 10 customers alone accounted for a disproportionate share of total transactions, pointing to a small, high-value, loyal customer base worth protecting.

Recommendations delivered:

  • Strengthen engagement in the highest-revenue countries (France and Germany) with localized campaigns.
  • Focus marketing spend on Senior and Adult segments — loyalty programs, premium bundles, and simplified purchase flows for older users.
  • Grow Youth revenue with starter-tier products, discounts, and social-first campaigns.
Caught during QA The "Total spending by purchase band" chart classified 100% of transactions as "High," with the Medium and Low bands showing zero — a clear sign the segmentation thresholds were misconfigured rather than a real finding. I've flagged this for correction; since this project only has the exported report (not the live Power BI file or dataset connection), the fix is documented here rather than shown, and I'd rebuild it properly given access to the source file again.
Case study 03

What actually makes a song a hit? — Spotify streams analysis

Python · pandas · matplotlib

A dataset of 953 of the most-streamed Spotify tracks, analyzed in Python to find out which songs, artists, and audio characteristics actually drive stream counts — and to test the common assumption that a song's "danceability" or tempo predicts its popularity.

953

8

3
data-quality issues found & cleaned
The 10 most-streamed tracks in the dataset. "Blinding Lights" and "Shape of You" lead by a wide margin, both well past 3.5 billion streams.
Tracks in the dataset by release year. Nearly 60% were released in 2022–2023, meaning this dataset reflects "recent hits," not a representative sample of all-time streaming — worth stating plainly rather than implying otherwise.
Correlation between each audio feature and stream count. Every feature — including danceability, energy, and tempo — is close to zero. None of Spotify's own audio metrics meaningfully predict how many streams a track gets.
Top artists by total streams across their tracks in the dataset. Bad Bunny and The Weeknd lead, each above 23 billion combined streams.
Caught during cleaning One row's "streams" field contained scraped text from other columns instead of a number (a parsing error at the source) — dropped after confirming it couldn't be recovered. Two tracks appeared twice with different stream counts for the same title and artist, most likely re-releases or an update strategy in the data collection — resolved by keeping the higher, more recent count. 94 tracks were missing a musical key value, left as missing rather than guessed, since fabricating a key would misrepresent the data.
Key takeaway None of the eight audio features in this dataset (danceability, energy, valence, tempo, etc.) correlate meaningfully with stream count. Whatever makes a song a hit — artist reach, playlist placement, virality, timing — it isn't captured by these audio characteristics alone. A useful reminder not to over-trust a "hit prediction" model built only on audio features.
Case study 04

AI response evaluation: rubric, ratings, and edits

Self-directed demo · mirrors paid eval work on Oneforma & Handshake

View project on GitHub →

My paid evaluation work is under NDA, so nothing from those platforms appears here. This is a self-built demo using the same core workflow: compare two AI responses against a rubric I define, score them with reasoning, then edit the weaker response into a corrected version — the three things I do daily as a rater.

Rubric used for every prompt below

  • Accuracy — is every claim correct?
  • Completeness — does it fully answer what was asked?
  • Clarity — is it easy to follow, with no unnecessary padding?
  • Tone — natural and appropriate for the question?
Prompt — factual: "What causes the seasons on Earth?"
Response A

The seasons happen because the Earth moves around the sun in a big circle every year, and depending on where the Earth is in that circle, different parts of the planet get more or less sunlight, and also the Earth is spinning while it orbits, and this spin combined with the orbit and the distance from the sun creates the seasons we experience throughout the year in different places.

Response B

Seasons are caused by Earth's axial tilt (about 23.5°), not its distance from the sun. As Earth orbits the sun, the tilt means different hemispheres receive more direct sunlight at different times of year — producing summer where sunlight hits more directly, and winter where it hits at a shallower angle.

AccuracyCompletenessClarityTone
Response A2/52/51/53/5
Response B5/55/55/55/5

Reasoning: Response A never names the actual cause (axial tilt) and implies distance-from-sun plays a role — a common misconception. It's also a single 70-word run-on sentence. Response B is correct, direct, and complete in three sentences.

Edited version of Response A

Seasons are caused by Earth's 23.5° axial tilt, not by changes in distance from the sun. As Earth orbits, the tilt causes each hemisphere to receive more direct sunlight for part of the year (summer) and less direct sunlight the rest of the year (winter).

Prompt — creative: "Write a two-sentence product description for a reusable water bottle."
Response A

This water bottle is great for staying hydrated. It is durable, reusable, and good for the environment.

Response B

Double-walled steel keeps drinks cold for 24 hours or hot for 12, so your coffee is still hot at your 2pm meeting. One bottle, refilled daily, replaces roughly 1,000 single-use plastic bottles a year.

AccuracyCompletenessClarityTone
Response A3/52/53/52/5
Response B4/55/55/55/5

Reasoning: Response A is generic boilerplate that could describe any product — no specific detail a buyer could act on. Response B gives concrete, checkable specs (temperature retention, time frames) and a vivid comparison, which is what a product description needs to do.

Edited version of Response A

Insulated stainless steel keeps water cold all day, even left in a hot car or gym bag. Refill it instead of buying bottled water, and it pays for itself within a couple of months.

What this demonstrates Defining criteria before scoring (not judging on vibes), giving reasoning that would hold up to a second rater checking my work, and turning a critique into a concrete edit rather than just a score — the same loop used in production rating and annotation work.
